No eclipse glasses?
Two fixes, both free.

Half of Scotland is asking the same question today. Here's the honest answer: it's too late for online orders — but there are free certified glasses at events tonight, and a completely safe viewer you can make in two minutes.

First, the hard rule. Only glasses marked ISO 12312-2 (printed on the frame) are safe for looking at the Sun. Sunglasses, smoked glass, CDs, phone screens and camera filters are not — a 91% eclipse is still full-strength sunlight, and eye damage is painless and permanent. No code on the frame, don't use them.

Fix 2 — make a safe viewer in two minutes

The pinhole projector

You never look at the Sun at all — you look at its projection, which is completely safe, and during the deep eclipse the projected image becomes a thin crescent. Kids can make it and kids love it.

  1. Take two pieces of card (cereal box works).
  2. Pierce a small round hole in one with a pin.
  3. Stand with your back to the Sun. Hold the pinhole card up over your shoulder.
  4. Let the sunlight pass through the hole onto the second card — there's the Sun, as a little disc of light. Tonight from 18:08 it becomes a bite, then a crescent.

Even lazier: a kitchen colander projects dozens of crescents at once. And any leafy tree does it for free — the gaps between leaves are natural pinholes, and the pavement underneath fills with crescents. That's the photo everyone remembers.

🌒 Look Up — Pinhole Eclipse Viewer

Eclipse tonight: 18:08 – 20:01. Deepest around 19:05. This sheet lets you watch it with your back to the Sun — completely safe, no glasses needed.

pierce this circle once with a pin

How to use it:

  1. Pierce the marked circle once with a pin or sharp pencil. Small and round beats big and ragged.
  2. Cut the sheet in half — the half with the hole is your projector, the blank half is your screen.
  3. Stand with your BACK to the Sun. Never look at the Sun directly.
  4. Hold the pinhole half up over your shoulder, and catch the spot of light on the blank half.
  5. That spot IS the Sun. From 18:08 watch a bite appear; by 19:05 it's a thin crescent.

Move the sheets further apart for a bigger (dimmer) image. A leafy tree does the same trick — look at the ground underneath it during the eclipse.
